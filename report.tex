% =========================================================================== ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
INT8 post-training quantization is a common way to make object detectors fast enough for drones,
and its accuracy cost is usually reported as one aggregate value. Aerial images are dominated by
small objects, which are often assumed to be more sensitive to reduced precision. We tested this
assumption in a pre-registered experiment. A YOLOv8n detector was fine-tuned on VisDrone2019-DET
and converted with TensorRT into FP32, FP16 and INT8 engines, with three INT8 calibration sets.
All engines were evaluated on the same 1,610 test-dev images, and the difference in relative
average precision loss between small and non-small objects was estimated with a paired
image-level bootstrap. INT8 reduced AP@[.5:.95] by 6.43\% (relative) for small objects and by
5.47\% for non-small objects. The difference, $D=+0.96$ percentage points, had a 95\% confidence
interval of $[-0.81,\,+3.03]$ ($[-0.65,\,+4.14]$ under a post-hoc cluster bootstrap). Because the
interval includes zero, the
pre-registered result is inconclusive: the data do not show that INT8 harms small objects more.
The difference was positive for all three calibration sets, but no interval excluded zero. The
data rule out a size-dependent penalty larger than about four percentage points, which is moderate
next to the overall INT8 loss of 5.4\%.
\end{abstract}

% =========================================================================== INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) increasingly run object detectors on board, where memory, energy
and latency budgets are tight. A standard way to meet these budgets is post-training quantization
(PTQ) to 8-bit integers (INT8). A network trained in 32-bit floating point (FP32) is converted
without retraining, using a small set of calibration images to estimate the value ranges of its
activations~\cite{jacob2018quantization,nagel2021whitepaper}. Toolchains
such as TensorRT make this conversion a routine export step for YOLO
detectors~\cite{ultralytics_tensorrt}, and its accuracy cost is usually reported as a single
change in mean average precision (mAP).

A single score can hide uneven effects. In image classification, compressed networks lose
accuracy disproportionately on a subset of examples~\cite{hooker2019forget}. In aerial
detection, the subset of concern is small objects. They make up most annotations in drone
benchmarks such as
VisDrone~\cite{zhu2022visdrone,cao2021visdrone} and are already the hardest to
detect~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. Two mechanisms make a larger INT8 penalty for small objects
plausible. First, a small object is represented by few activations, and their resolution can
suffer when large outlier values dominate the quantization range, a known difficulty of PTQ for
detectors~\cite{nagel2021whitepaper,kim2026inlier}. Second, a localization error of one pixel
lowers the intersection over union (IoU) of a small box much more than that of a large box.

If small objects lose more, aggregate mAP overstates how suitable INT8 is for drones, where small
objects such as pedestrians often matter most. If they do not, size-specific countermeasures are
not needed for quantization. Detection benchmarks commonly report accuracy by COCO size
group~\cite{lin2014coco}, but the PTQ studies cited here propose or compare methods using
aggregate accuracy and do not test whether the loss depends on object size.

\textbf{Research question:} \emph{In aerial imagery, is the relative loss in detection
accuracy caused by INT8 post-training quantization of a lightweight YOLO detector larger for
small objects than for non-small objects?}

\emph{Variables, hypotheses and prediction.} The independent variable is numerical precision:
an FP32 baseline versus an INT8 treatment, with an FP16 control. Object size is an observed
moderator with two levels, small (box area below $32^2$ pixels) and non-small. The dependent
variable is COCO AP averaged over IoU thresholds 0.50--0.95 (AP@[.5:.95]) per size group. The
checkpoint, input, inference settings, test images,
hardware and software are held constant. The test statistic $D$, defined in
Section~\ref{sec:method}, is the relative AP loss of small objects minus that of non-small
objects. The hypotheses are $H_0\!: D\le 0$ and $H_1\!: D>0$. If $H_1$ holds, we predict that the
95\% confidence interval (CI) of $D$ lies entirely above zero.

% =========================================================================== RELATED WORK
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\emph{Quantization.} Jacob et al.~\cite{jacob2018quantization} formalized integer-arithmetic
inference with affine quantization of weights and activations. Nagel et
al.~\cite{nagel2021whitepaper} and Krishnamoorthi~\cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantizing} review
practical PTQ, including per-channel weight quantization and calibration of activation ranges. Wu
et al.~\cite{wu2020integer} compare calibration methods for PTQ and find that max calibration,
which our toolchain uses, gives inconsistent accuracy, with large drops for networks with outlier
activations. They report one aggregate metric per model. INT8 can also be used
during training~\cite{zhu2020int8}, but this changes the trained model and is outside our scope.

\emph{Detectors and small objects.} Kim et al.~\cite{kim2026inlier} propose a new PTQ method that
handles outlier activations in object detectors. We instead measure a standard deployment
toolchain~\cite{ultralytics_tensorrt}. The VisDrone
benchmark~\cite{zhu2022visdrone} and the VisDrone-DET2021 challenge
results~\cite{cao2021visdrone} show that small, densely packed objects remain difficult, and
Akyon et al.~\cite{akyon2022sahi} address them with slicing-aided inference. Whether object
size defines a subset of the kind Hooker et al.~\cite{hooker2019forget} found for compressed
classifiers is the question studied here.

% =========================================================================== METHOD
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research Design}
We ran a quantitative controlled experiment because the question concerns a measurable causal
effect of one manipulated factor, precision. A TensorRT FP32 engine is the baseline, an INT8 engine the
treatment and an FP16 engine a control. The control is needed because the toolchain runs the
non-quantized layers of the INT8 engine in FP16. The design is paired: all conditions come from
the same checkpoint and ONNX graph and are evaluated on the same images with the same settings.
The design, metrics, size groups and analysis were fixed in a pre-registration dated 7 Oct.\
2026, before any model was trained. It is published with the code and a deviation
log~\cite{hussaini2026researchmethodology}.

For each object-size group $g$, the relative AP loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RelDrop}_{g} =
\frac{\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{FP32}}_{g} - \mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{INT8}}_{g}}
{\mathrm{AP}^{\mathrm{FP32}}_{g}}.
\label{eq:relative-ap-drop}
\end{equation}
The primary contrast is
\begin{equation}
D = \mathrm{RelDrop}_{\mathrm{small}} - \mathrm{RelDrop}_{\mathrm{non\text{-}small}}.
\label{eq:size-interaction}
\end{equation}
We use the relative loss because small objects start from a much lower AP. The same proportional
harm then gives a smaller absolute drop, so an absolute comparison would be biased against $H_1$.

\subsection{Dataset and Inclusion Criteria}
We used VisDrone2019-DET~\cite{zhu2022visdrone}, a standard drone benchmark dominated by small
objects, with 6,471 training, 548 validation and 1,610
test-dev images. The training split was used for fine-tuning and INT8 calibration, the validation
split for checkpoint selection and development, and test-dev only for the final evaluation. The
dataset archives were verified by SHA-256.

Annotations are converted to YOLO format for training and to COCO JSON for evaluation. All images
of every split are used, without sampling. An annotation is included only if its VisDrone score
is one. Score-zero annotations, which are the ignored regions and the ``others'' category (2,180
and 265 in test-dev), are excluded, so detections on ignored regions count as false positives in
every condition. Absolute AP is therefore not directly comparable with the official VisDrone
toolkit~\cite{cao2021visdrone}, but the paired comparison is unaffected. Boxes are clipped to the
image, and boxes with a width or height below one pixel are discarded (one box, in the training
split). Eight automatic integrity checks passed, for example that every image decodes, that the
YOLO and COCO labels contain identical boxes and that no image appears in two splits.

Object size is the area of the clipped ground-truth box in original image pixels. Following
COCO~\cite{lin2014coco}, small objects have an area below $32^2=1{,}024$ pixels and non-small
objects an area of at least $1{,}024$ pixels. The secondary groups are medium
($32^2 \leq \mathrm{area} < 96^2$) and large ($\mathrm{area} \geq 96^2=9{,}216$ pixels). Test-dev
contains 75,102 objects, of which 67.7\% are small, 29.1\% medium and 3.2\% large (60.5\%, 34.0\%
and 5.5\% in the training split). Size and class are related. Pedestrians, people and motors make
up 58.5\% of small but only 14.4\% of non-small objects, and cars, vans, trucks and buses 38.3\%
and 82.1\%, respectively. Size-specific evaluation follows the COCO rule: ground truths outside a
size range, detections matched to them, and unmatched detections outside the range are ignored
for that range.

\subsection{Procedure}
The experiment was run in five steps.
(1)~A pilot run (three epochs on 10\% of the training split, evaluated on validation) tested the
whole pipeline. Its results are not used.
(2)~YOLOv8n~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}, the smallest YOLOv8 variant and a typical choice for
embedded hardware, was initialized from COCO-pretrained weights and fine-tuned once on the
training split at $640 \times 640$ for 100 epochs, with batch size 16, random seed 0 and
deterministic training. Other options used the Ultralytics defaults, without hyper-parameter
search. The best checkpoint on the validation split was frozen.
(3)~The checkpoint was exported to ONNX with a static input of $1 \times 3 \times 640 \times 640$,
and a hash check confirmed that this graph was identical for all conditions. It was converted to
FP32, FP16 and INT8 TensorRT engines with the documented Ultralytics
workflow~\cite{ultralytics_tensorrt}. For INT8, NVIDIA ModelOpt inserts explicit
quantize/dequantize nodes with max calibration, and unquantized layers run in FP16. Three
calibration sets were sampled, one per seed (0, 1 and 2), each with 512 training images drawn
uniformly at random. Calibration and evaluation use the same $640 \times 640$ letterbox
preprocessing. Seed 0 is the primary INT8 condition, and seeds 1 and 2 are robustness checks.
(4)~All engines were evaluated on the validation split, which was the only split used for
development and debugging.
(5)~Each engine was evaluated on test-dev exactly once (12 Oct.\ 2026), and the per-image match
records were stored for the bootstrap.

\subsection{Measures and Implementation Checks}
The PyTorch checkpoint and all engines use the same inference settings:
confidence threshold 0.001, non-maximum suppression (NMS) IoU threshold 0.7, at most 500
detections per image, batch size 1, and square $640 \times 640$ input. The primary measure is
COCO-style AP averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 (AP@[.5:.95]), because its strict
thresholds also capture the localization errors that motivate the hypothesis. AP at IoU 0.50
(AP@0.5), which mainly reflects whether objects are found, is secondary. Both are reported
overall and by size group. Predictions are matched to ground truth with pycocotools, and our AP
recomputation must agree with pycocotools to within $10^{-9}$. We also compare the PyTorch FP32
checkpoint with the TensorRT FP32 engine, and our results with the Ultralytics validation
metrics.

\subsection{Analysis Plan}
AP is computed from detections ranked across the whole test set, so there are no independent
per-image AP values for a classical paired test. We therefore use a paired nonparametric
bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} with the image as the resampling unit. It draws 2,000
resamples of the 1,610 test-dev images with replacement (random seed 0), which gives a Monte
Carlo error of about 0.06 percentage points for the interval limits. Every condition uses the same resampled image indices, which keeps the
pairing, and AP is recomputed by size group from the stored match records without re-matching.

The confirmatory statistic is $D$ in~\eqref{eq:size-interaction} for FP32 versus INT8 seed 0 on
AP@[.5:.95], with a two-sided 95\% percentile bootstrap confidence interval. If the lower bound is
above zero, $H_0$ is rejected in favor of a greater relative loss for small objects. If the
interval contains zero, the result is inconclusive. If the upper bound is below zero, the result
is contrary to $H_1$. Because $H_1$ is directional, this rule corresponds to a one-sided test at
$\alpha=0.025$.

The percentile bootstrap makes no normality assumption about $D$, a ratio-based quantity with an
unknown sampling distribution. It does assume that test images are independent and that the
resampled AP is well defined. We check that AP is finite in every resample and inspect the
bootstrap distribution for skew and bias. The image was chosen as the unit because VisDrone
test-dev file names do not reliably identify video sequences. This was decided from the labels
alone, before any model output existed. Dependence between images of the same scene is examined
with a post-hoc cluster bootstrap.

All other comparisons are exploratory, reported with unadjusted confidence intervals and not used
for hypothesis testing:
absolute AP drops, AP@0.5, small, medium and large AP, calibration seeds 1 and 2, and the FP16
control. The INT8 result counts as directionally robust if $D$ has the same sign for all three
calibration seeds. If FP16 showed a similar size pattern, the effect could not be attributed to
INT8 alone.

\subsection{Setup, Deviations and Ethics}
All main-study training, engine builds and evaluations ran on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080
(16\,GB) in one fixed environment: Python 3.11.9, PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128, Ultralytics 8.4.174 and
TensorRT 11.3.0.99. Ultralytics and TensorRT match the pre-registration. Python and PyTorch differ
from the versions in the original README (3.12 and 2.14.1), and every run log from the first
training onward records the versions actually used. The pre-registered latency benchmark, a
secondary outcome, was not implemented, so we report the inference times recorded by
Ultralytics, as the pre-registered fallback allows. These deviations (D1, D2), a re-run of the
pilot on validation only (D3) and the post-hoc cluster bootstrap (D4) are recorded in a deviation
log written after the experiment from the run logs. None of them affects the confirmatory
analysis. The study uses
only a public research benchmark~\cite{zhu2022visdrone}, involves no human participants, and does
not redistribute images or use them to identify people.

\subsection{Exploratory Comparisons}
Table~\ref{tab:contrasts} lists the exploratory contrasts. The point estimate of $D$ was positive
for all three calibration seeds ($+0.96$, $+1.83$ and $+1.19$ percentage points), which meets the
pre-registered criterion for being directionally robust. All three intervals included zero,
however. The overall relative loss
varied more between seeds (5.4\%, 7.4\% and 7.0\%) than $D$ did. On AP@0.5, $D$ was close to zero
for every seed. The absolute contrast $D_{\mathrm{abs}}$ was negative for every seed, with
intervals excluding zero. In AP points, small objects lost less than non-small objects.

The FP16 engine lost only 0.37\% of overall AP, so TensorRT conversion itself costs little
accuracy and the INT8 loss comes mainly from quantization. The FP16 $D$ was $+0.42$ percentage points
with an interval just above zero, while its $D_{\mathrm{abs}}$ was essentially zero. Because the
primary INT8 interval includes zero, the pre-registered FP16 attribution rule is not triggered.

\subsection{Assumption and Implementation Checks}
All 2,000 resamples gave finite AP values for
every condition and size group. The bootstrap
distribution of $D$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:d-bootstrap}) is unimodal and close to normal (skewness 0.06,
excess kurtosis 0.01), with a standard error of 0.99 percentage points, and 12.5\% of the
resamples had $D\le0$. Its mean exceeds the point estimate by 0.16 percentage points. A
normal-approximation interval (point estimate $\pm1.96$ standard errors,
$[-0.99,\,+2.91]$) also contains zero, so the decision does not depend on the interval method. As
a post-hoc sensitivity check for dependence between images of the same scene, a cluster bootstrap
with 2,000 resamples resampled the 61 filename prefixes, which we treat as possible scenes,
instead of single images. It widened the interval of $D$ to
$[-0.65,\,+4.14]$ (standard error 1.20 percentage points) without changing the decision, and the
FP16 interval stayed above zero ($[+0.09,\,+0.90]$).

Our AP matched pycocotools to within $2\times10^{-16}$ for every
engine. TensorRT FP32 and PyTorch FP32 differed by less than $10^{-4}$ AP@[.5:.95] (0--1 scale) in every
size group, so the TensorRT FP32 engine is a faithful baseline. Overall AP differed from the
Ultralytics validator by 0.0028--0.0029 (0--1 scale) for every TensorRT engine. This offset comes from a
different AP implementation and is nearly constant across conditions, so it does not affect the
paired contrasts.

\subsection{Latency}
The per-image inference times recorded by Ultralytics during test-dev evaluation, an
uncontrolled and descriptive measure, were 0.77\,ms for FP32, 0.42\,ms for FP16 and
0.69--0.71\,ms for the three INT8 engines.

% =========================================================================== DISCUSSION
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The hypothesis $H_1$ was not supported. This inconclusive result is not evidence that the two
groups are affected equally, but the interval does bound the effect. A size-dependent penalty
larger than about 4 percentage points (upper limits $+3.03$ under the image bootstrap and $+4.14$
under the cluster bootstrap) is not compatible with the data. This corresponds to a small-object
relative loss above roughly 8.5--9.6\% against 5.5\% for non-small objects. This bound is moderate next
to the overall INT8 loss of 5.4\%. Hooker et
al.~\cite{hooker2019forget} found disproportionate losses on subsets of examples for compressed
classifiers, but we could not establish such a subset by object size. For this detector, the
aggregate INT8 loss is a reasonable first guide to the loss on small objects, although
deployments that depend on small objects should still evaluate them separately.

The relative and absolute views disagree. Small objects lost slightly more in proportion, while
non-small objects lost about three times more AP points (1.39 vs.\ 0.43), so whether
quantization ``hurts small objects more'' depends on the scale. The two mechanisms from the
introduction also predict different patterns across IoU thresholds. Coarser activations would
tend to make small objects disappear, which should show up at IoU 0.5, while localization jitter
would mainly hurt small objects at high IoU thresholds. We found $D$ near zero on AP@0.5 but
slightly positive on AP@[.5:.95]. This is weakly consistent with the localization mechanism, but
the difference is well within the uncertainty.

The three calibration seeds share the same model and test images, so their common direction is
not an independent replication. The choice of calibration set changed the overall INT8 loss by
two percentage points, more than the size contrast itself. This agrees with Wu et
al.~\cite{wu2020integer}, who found max calibration inconsistent across networks with outlier
activations. Outlier-sensitive calibration~\cite{kim2026inlier} is a plausible cause of these
differences, but our experiment does not test it.

The FP16 $D$ had an interval just above zero under both bootstraps, but its small-object loss is
only 0.05 AP points, so this relative difference is fragile. Very small objects may be sensitive to any reduction in
precision, or the result may be a chance finding among several uncorrected exploratory
intervals.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\emph{Internal validity.} Precision is the only manipulated factor. TensorRT does, however, choose
kernels and layer fusions per precision, and the INT8 engine runs its unquantized layers in FP16.
The FP16 control bounds this toolchain effect but cannot fully separate it from quantization.

\emph{Construct validity.} Object size is observed, not assigned. Small and non-small objects
differ in class composition (Section~\ref{sec:method}), so $D$ measures size and class together.
Small objects also start from a low AP of 6.62 AP points, which makes relative losses noisy. The
uncontrolled
timings show no speed-up of INT8 over FP16 at batch size 1, so the study cannot show
that the accuracy loss buys faster inference.

\emph{Conclusion validity.} The bootstrap captures variation across test images, not from model
training or hardware. The cluster bootstrap handles same-scene correlation only coarsely, because
five filename prefixes hold 71\% of the images. With a CI width of about 4--5 percentage points,
the study could detect only a moderate or larger size effect. The pre-registration was not deposited in a
time-stamped registry, and the public repository was first committed after the test-dev analysis,
so its date rests on our own record.

\emph{External validity.} We used one YOLOv8n model, one dataset and a $640\times640$ full-image
input, which downscales the original images. Slicing-aided inference~\cite{akyon2022sahi}
enlarges small objects, so the findings should not be generalized to tiled pipelines, larger
detectors or other quantization toolchains.

% =========================================================================== CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We tested whether INT8 post-training quantization of YOLOv8n causes a greater relative accuracy
loss for small than for non-small objects on VisDrone2019-DET. The relative AP loss was 6.43\%
for small and 5.47\% for non-small objects, a difference of 0.96 percentage points (95\% CI
$[-0.81,\,+3.03]$), so a greater loss for small objects was not established. The consistent
direction across three calibration seeds did not resolve this uncertainty. In absolute
AP points, small objects lost less than non-small objects, with exploratory intervals excluding
zero. The FP16 control lost
almost no accuracy, so the overall loss of 5--7\% comes mainly from INT8 quantization, but the size
pattern is not necessarily specific to INT8.

Future work should test more models and datasets and measure inference speed under a controlled
protocol. Size and class could be separated by estimating $D$ within one class, such as cars,
which are numerous in both groups (15,112 small and 12,962 non-small), or by rescaling test
images so that the same objects cross the size threshold.
